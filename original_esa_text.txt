Multimodal Wearable Stress Detection Using Deep Learning: A Comparative Study of Signal Analysis and Real-Time Monitoring
Authors: Praneet Raj, Aarav Shukla, Saanvy Panda
Abstract
The use of a wearable device for detecting stress provides a means of carrying out continuous health monitoring and enables the identification of changes associated with stress. The differences in physiological responses from person to person and the complexity of the temporal relationships between the signals pose considerable difficulties for reliable stress detection. This project proposes a deep learning approach based on multiple physiological modalities for the detection of acute stress using electrocardiogram (ECG), photoplethysmogram (PPG), and skin temperature signals. A binary classification system was developed using the WESAD (Wearable Stress and Affect Detection) dataset to distinguish between non-stress conditions and acute stress caused by the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST). The preprocessing procedure involved resampling the signals to a common 128 Hz time base, applying filtering, normalising the data on a per-subject basis, segmenting the signals using sliding windows, and extracting physiological features. Four deep learning architectures and three classical machine learning classifiers were assessed, and their performance was evaluated using 15-fold Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation in order to examine generalisation to new subjects who had not been included in the training. Of the deep learning models, the Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN) achieved a mean accuracy of 76.50%, an F1-score of 0.6578, and a mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.8665, with a reported model inference latency of 2.57 ms when run on a CPU. Although the Random Forest classifier obtained marginally higher mean accuracy and F1-score, the ST-GCN provided a good balance between performance and computational efficiency. The trained model was exported to ONNX and then validated against its PyTorch implementation. The resulting inference pipeline forms a basis for integration with an ESP32-based wearable system and a remote monitoring dashboard. The approach outlined has the potential to support further development in the areas of real-time stress monitoring, historical analysis, and telemedicine applications, provided that hardware integration and additional validation are carried out.
Keywords: Wearable Stress Detection, Multimodal Physiological Signal Fusion, Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks, Subject-Independent Classification, Leave-One-Subject-Out Cross-Validation, Heart Rate Variability, Edge AI Deployment, WESAD Dataset.
1. Introduction
1.1 Background and Motivation
Stress is a normal physiological and psychological response to stress. Although short-term stress may be a normal part of daily life, chronic or recurrent stress may affect an individual’s well-being and daily functioning. Conventional approaches to stress assessment primarily depend on self-reported questionnaires, observations or periodic assessments which may not be able to track changes in physiological responses continuously. This calls for the need of objective, accessible and continuous approaches for stress monitoring.
Wearable technology along with machine learning are a good way to address this challenge. Physiological signals such as the electrocardiogram (ECG), the photoplethysmogram (PPG), and the skin temperature provide information about the changes in the cardiovascular activity and other bodily responses related to stress. By analysing these signals together, machine learning algorithms can find patterns that may help differentiate stress-related physiological responses from non-stress conditions.
However, reliable stress detection remains a challenging task as physiological responses differ between people, multiple signals may have complex temporal relationships, and models trained on one group of subjects may not generalise well to previously unseen subjects. These challenges motivate the use of multimodal signal analysis, advanced deep learning architectures, and subject independent evaluation.
1.2 Real-World Importance of the Problem
Continuous stress monitoring has potential applications in personal wellness, workplace well-being, remote health monitoring and telemedicine. A wearable system capable of analysing physiological signals could assist users in identifying periods of increased stress and in reviewing their physiological trends over time. Remote monitoring interfaces could also give authorised health care professionals the ability to view previous measurements and discuss relevant patterns with patients.
Physiological monitoring gives another source of information about how the body is responding, unlike systems that are based only on subjective reports. But these physiological changes are not unique to stress and can be caused by exercise, illness, environmental conditions, or other factors. Thus, automated stress detection should be used as a supportive monitoring tool and not as a stand-alone diagnostic method.
The usefulness of a system relies not only on its classification efficiency but also on its computational efficiency, dependable signal acquisition and result communication capabilities through an interface that is easy to use. Because of these facts, the area of wearable stress identification becomes a significant sphere of using machine learning and embedded technology.
1.3 Why Arduino-Based Hardware and Machine Learning Are Suitable
The usefulness of a system relies not only on its classification efficiency but also on its computational efficiency, dependable signal acquisition and result communication capabilities through an interface that is easy to use. Because of these facts, the area of wearable stress identification becomes a significant sphere of using machine learning and embedded technology.

Multimodal methods are more capable of integrating information from different types of physiological data.

1.4 Scope of the Project
This project aims to conduct research on the application of deep learning for the detection of anxiety using a combination of physiological signals and to prepare the model for use in a wearable system.
The data in the machine learning process is derived from the WESAD data set, which has four categories of signals, namely, ECG, PPG, and temperature of the skin. These signals are preprocessed and synchronized to the uniform sample rate of 128 Hz and subsequently normalized and divided into segments of 3 seconds each with 50% overlapping. The classification task includes separating the non-stressful cases, which consist of baseline and amusement states, from acute stress cases generated by the use of TSST.
Four methods of deep learning—1D-CNN, BiLSTM, Temporal Transformer, and Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Neural Network (ST-GCN)—are tested against comparable machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest, XGBoost, and Support Vector Machine (SVM). The performance of each model is evaluated through a 15-fold Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation technique that tests the model's ability to generalize for excluded subjects. In the course of evaluating the models, classification metrics such as accuracy, F1-score, and AUC-ROC, as well as computational latency are taken into consideration. Based on the analysis of performance and inference time, the ST-GCN architecture is selected as the best. Once trained, the model is converted to the ONNX format and validated against the results from PyTorch.
The overall system incorporates an inference pipeline in functionality and design into an ESP32-based wearable and a remote monitoring dashboard. The dashboard has a potential of presenting stress_metrics assessment, body readings captured, and history. It should, however, be noted that this hardware and application ability is a design feature and not an outcome of the conducted machine learning evaluation.
2. Literature Review
Gedan et al. [1] introduced a framework based on machine learning for identifying mental stress utilizing wearable physiological sensors along with a low-cost device inspired by Arduino. The research gathered physiological data from two hundred undergraduate students from India using sensors measuring electrocardiogram (ECG), galvanic skin response (GSR), and skin temperature (ST) during four tasks including watching videos with funny content, conducting tests requiring arithmetic calculations, listening to favorite songs, and taking Stroop Color Word Test. The authors indicated 31 physiological features and processed signals using signal filtering, Fast Independent Component Analysis (Fast ICA), and feature scaling prior to applying nine machine learning algorithms for training them. Both analyses were performed in modalities of individual-signal and multimodal system for classification the stress and non-stress states. The Extreme Gradient Boosting model obtained the maximum accuracy of 96.17% subsequently followed by Random Forest with an accuracy of 96.03%. The validation carried out on the data sets of SWELL-KW and WESAD showed 92.38% and 94.21% accuracies respectively. Moreover, the implementation of a real-time pilot on ten other participants gave an accuracy of 97.5% with timely issues taking around 1.2 seconds. However, the study focused on healthy undergraduate participants between the ages of 20 and 26 thereby limiting the study’s generalizability to real-world situations. Additionally, the use of hand-crafted features and conventional machine learning algorithms leaves room for the possibility of employing deep-learning techniques in this field.
In 2019, Behinaein et al. [2] introduced a deep learning model that integrates convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with a transformer encoder to help with stress identification utilizing ECG signals. The proposed approach was examined using the WESAD and SWELL-KW datasets through leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation. ECG signals were conditioned with a fifth order Butterworth high pass filter at a cutoff frequency of 0.5 Hz and downsampled to 256 Hz. The dataset was also normalized according to a subject-specific z-score normalization approach. The deep learning architecture was composed of two convolutional layers to extract features, a transformer encoder with multi-heads self-attention to process temporal information, and fully-connected layers for binary stress classifications.. In contrast to traditional methods, which depend on routine feature extraction and selection, the approach uses raw ECG data to allow model training without any supervision. The initial tests showed promising results of 80.4% accuracy and 69.7% F1 score on WESAD and 58.1% accuracy and 58.8% F1 score on SWELL-KW, without fine-tuning. Subsequent fine-tuning, using only 10% of the data from the subject under testing, has led to statistics of 91.1% accuracy and 71.6% F1 score on the above-mentioned datasets. These exceptional results indicate problems with generalization to previously unseen subjects. An interesting direction of the research is looking into various physiological modalities, which could facilitate understanding of the discussed problem.
In their research, Rashid and his colleagues [3] introduced the SELF-CARE framework. This framework is sensor fusion-based and utilizes physiological indicators provided by wearables in order to assess stress levels of users. The authors tackled challenges related to noise in physiological data and the shortcomings of static sensor fusion techniques aimed at adjusting to varying sensing conditions. The framework detects noise contexts of operation and selects the proper type of sensors and classifiers to use. The testing was carried out with the use of the WESAD corpus, which consists of 15 subjects using RespiBAN and Empatica E4 devices for stress monitoring. Accelerometer modalities differentiate motion noise in wrist-worn devices. At the same time, electromyography (EMG) modalities distinguish muscle contraction noise in chest-worn devices. Various classifiers for each type of modality are integrated in a context classification way in combination with Kalman filter to implement temporal late data fusion. The proposed technique was tested, using LOSO cross-validation and gave 94.12% accuracy and macro F1 score of 92.93% for binary classification based on wrist sensor data, while chest sensors reached 93.68% accuracy. Results can demonstrate how it is possible to select the appropriate modality based on noise type. Nonetheless, the framework uses conventional classification and not deep learning methods. Also, the framework is tested only on WESAD which requires further development towards cross-dataset usage.
Kim et al. [4] introduced their Shuffled ECA-Net framework as part of several multimodal procedures. The study combined physiological signals by wearing a customized device that remotely obtains three types of signals, namely electrocardiograms (ECGs), respiratory signals (RESPs) and electrogastrograms (EGGs). Stress conditions were assessed through collecting saliva samples and measuring cortisol levels. KIm et al. collected data from a group of 20 subjects which is narrowed down from an initial group of 26 volunteers due to some leakage of important data. The proposed method works as follows: the system employs three separate 1D-CNN networks for each kind of signal and then joins the output through the various shuffled ECA module, which takes care of the relationships between the modalities. The intrahost accuracy of 91.6%, F1 of 91.4%, and AUROC of 96.4% show high efficacy of the method while intersubject results are poor. The initial analysis demonstrates how difficult it is to effectively apply the developed approach to new participants.
Yildiz and Subasi [5] introduced PAMG-AT, which is short for Physiological Attention Multi-Graph with Adaptive Topology, which is a graph neural network that is processed hierarchically. The main purpose of this study based on WESAD was to determine stress through various sensor arrangements such as using only signals from a chest, wrist, as well as both sensors, and how this affects the results of the study. The output files are the nodes of the graph, while the edges represent interaction between different signals- multiple gathering signals in one place. The architecture used Graph Attention Networks (GAT) for learning intra-signal relationships and inter-signal relationships similar to the Transformer encoder for temporal dependency learning across continuous windows of data. During the 15-time cross-validation process, the model displayed an accuracy rate of 94.59%, 91.76%, and 92.80% for chest signals, wrist signals, and hybrid configuration respectively. By performing attention-weight analyses, it was determined that ECG-EDA coupling was the most significant inter-signal relationship in the chest configuration. The findings also point to high variability of performance among the subjects with unusual physiological reactions to stress. While on one hand, the architecture has over 4 million of trainable parameters, which may pose a problem from the standpoint of computational efficiency, the hybrid configuration offered even lower accuracy rate than that of the chest-only configuration, indicating that adding more sensors does not necessarily improve accuracy. Thus, there is a need for trying out different multimodal architectures with physiological relationship combinations in order to support computational efficiency while preserving reliable performance with regard to the subjects’ results.
Research gap
Although existing studies have demonstrated promising results in wearable stress detection using conventional machine learning, deep learning, attention mechanisms, and multimodal sensor fusion, several research gaps remain:
Generalizability issues for new subjects: Many models show a drastic drop in the performance when tested on unknown subjects. This signifies that it is important to conduct a more robust evaluation out and to develop models capable of generalizing between different physiological responses.
Failure to adequately model the multimodal relationship: Some methods depend on a unimodal physiological signal, while others use multimodal signals but do not adequately account for the relationships between them temporally and physiologically. More research is needed on the design of architectures which can successfully learn complementary information from various physiological signals.
Computational complexity: Complicated architectures such as graph attention models may require more computational power. More computationally efficient models are required for wearable sensing and edge deployment applications.
Practicality in realistic settings: A lot of research is based on either experimental setups or one benchmarking data set. More testing for practicality is needed to demonstrate robustness and real world applicability under varying population, data set, sensor conditions, and real world activities.
Accuracy versus reliable evaluation: Good results under individual testing may not guarantee reliable results for unseen subjects. In future studies, subject-independent testing and other measures like F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, and inference time must be considered over accuracy.
In order to fill the existing gap, the current research work focuses on the comparison between machine learning models and deep learning models for physiological stress detection using ECG, PPG, and skin temperature data from WESAD. This research makes use of LOSO cross-validation to analyze the generalizability across subjects and examines the performance as well as latency.
3. Problem Statement
An unseen individual should be able to have his/her stress levels detected from a signal that is noisily acquired and processed using energy-efficient computations of the embedded device. Most of the existing approaches tend to be single-modal, depend on manual feature engineering, or are computationally expensive and cannot perform well when evaluated subject-independently. Thus, the goal of this study is to see if a lightweight multimodal model, using only ECG, PPG and skin temperature, can detect stress in unseen individuals.
4. Objectives
To develop a multimodal stress detection framework using ECG, PPG, and skin temperature signals from the WESAD dataset.
To preprocess physiological signals, extract relevant features, and generate suitable input windows for stress classification.
To implement and compare conventional machine learning models, including Random Forest, XGBoost, and SVM, with deep learning architectures such as 1D-CNN, BiLSTM, Temporal Transformer, and ST-GCN.
To evaluate model performance using Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation to assess generalization to unseen individuals.
To compare classification performance using accuracy, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, and ROC-AUC.
To evaluate computational efficiency by measuring model inference latency and identifying a suitable model for deployment.
To export the selected model to ONNX format and establish a foundation for integration with wearable sensors, an inference gateway, and a monitoring dashboard.

Software tools and libraries
Data collection method and dataset description
Model Training was performed on WESAD (Wearable Stress and Affect Detection) Dataset consisting of data recorded from 15 subjects wearing wearable chest and wrist sensors. In our case, three channels were chosen to represent the future hardware solution, namely, ECG, PPG and Temperature. The other channels from WESAD dataset, such as EDA/GSR and respiration, were omitted during model training.
Two classes are employed for classification:
•	Class 0 — non-stress state: Baseline and amusement conditions
•	Class 1 — acute stress state: Trier Social Stress Test (TSST) condition
Other states, such as transient states, were not considered in the present study. The result of the developed pipeline consists of 22,192 three-second signal windows of the 15 subjects
ESP32 sensors will be used for real-time data acquisition in the next step of the project. WESAD Dataset, but not the live data obtained from the ESP32 sensor, was used to train and validate the presented models
Data Preprocessing
Since the physiological signals are sampled at different rates, the raw physiological signals were first resampled to have a common sampling rate of 128 Hz before segmentation.
The preprocessing steps include:
Signal Extraction: ECG signal was extracted from WESAD chest sensor, whereas PPG and temperature were extracted from the WESAD wrist and chest sensors.
Resampling: ECG was down-sampled from 700 Hz to 128 Hz, PPG signal was up-sampled from 64 Hz to 128 Hz using cubic spline interpolation, and temperature was up-sampled from 4 Hz to 128 Hz using linear interpolation.
Noise Reduction: An IIR notch filter operating at 50 Hz was applied. ECG signal was bandpass filtered at 0.5 to 40 Hz, while PPG signal was bandpass filtered at 0.5 to 5 Hz. Temperature signal was not filtered.
Normalization: z-score normalization was performed on a subject basis.
Window Segmentation: Three seconds long segments with 384 samples in each channel were obtained from the physiological signals with 50% overlap and a 192-sample step size.
Label Assignment: Binary label was assigned to each window by majority voting among sample labels. Ambiguous windows were removed from the analysis dataset.
The deep learning pipeline provides an input tensor of shape (N, 3, 384), where N is the number of windows, 3 corresponds to physiological signals, and 384 to the sample size for each signal. While the classical ML pipeline works with physiological features.
ML model selection and architecture
Four deep learning architectures and three classical machine learning models were evaluated:
Deep Learning - 1D-CNN, BiLSTM, Temporal Transformer, ST-GCN
Classical ML - Random Forest, XGBoost, SVM
The Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN) was chosen for its deep learning model for its ability to strike the right balance between classification efficiency and inference speed.
Graph nodes for ST-GCN model ECG, PPG, and skin temperature as three graphs. A dynamic adjacency matrix is obtained based on the Pearson correlation values between these three signals in each window. Graph convolutions capture relationship between physiological channels while temporal convolutions learn from the signal window.

The models were trained and validated using a 15-fold Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation technique. In every fold, data for one subject was held out for testing while data for other 14 subjects was used for training and validation purposes. Training dataset was again split into training and validation datasets using 80:20 ratio. The method tests the generalization capability of the model for unseen subjects..
AdamW optimization algorithm was used for model training with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and weight decay of 0.01. Class weighted cross entropy loss function was used to solve class imbalance between the stressed and non-stressed classes. Learning rate schedule used was cosine annealing. Early stopping based on validation loss was used to minimize overfitting. The patience of early stopping is 10–15 epochs..
Accuracy, F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, and ROC-AUC were used to evaluate the models. Also, inference latency was calculated for determining the suitability of the model for real-time usage. During the single-fold experiment conducted initially, ST-GCN model was trained up to 100 epochs. While other models were trained up to a different number of epochs based on their validation accuracy. Final results were derived after conducting 15-fold LOSO experiment.
Model optimization and edge deployment
The trained ST-GCN model was exported from PyTorch to ONNX to simplify the deployment process and eliminate the use of PyTorch at inference time. The ONNX model was verified by comparing the outputs produced by the ONNX and PyTorch models using out-of-sample data. The maximum output difference was 4.77 x 10-6, which is less than the required tolerance of 10-4.

9.Experimental Setup
9.1 Test Environment and Evaluation Dataset
The experiments were run on the WESAD (Wearable Stress and Affect Detection) dataset, which included the physiological measurements of 15 participants. ECG, PPG, and skin temperature signals were chosen to build the classifier. The baseline and amusement signals were classified into the non-stress category, whereas TSST measurements were classified as the acute stress category.
Training the model took place on a local machine that had an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 GPU. For programming purposes, Python and PyTorch along with corresponding libraries for signal processing and machine learning were used. The dataset was pre-processed, normalized, and segmented into 22,192 segments with 3 seconds of physiological data overlapped by 50%.
Model evaluation was carried out by employing 15-fold Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation. In every fold, the recording of one subject was held out for test, whereas 14 other subjects were used to train and validate the model. The adoption of this subject independent protocol was necessary to measure the generalization ability of the model on unseen subjects.
This experimentation was carried out based on the pre-recorded data set of WESAD rather than the live measurement obtained from ESP32 hardware. Benchmarking for CPU Inference was carried out separately.
9.2 Performance Metrics
The models were evaluated using several performance criteria to measure accuracy, detection ability, and efficiency.
Accuracy is the fraction of true classifications over the total number of samples. Precision is the fraction of samples predicted to be stress that are actually stress, while recall (also known as sensitivity) is the fraction of stress samples that are actually detected as stress. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Specificity is the fraction of samples predicted not to be stress that are not actually stress, and ROC-AUC is the ability of the classifier to differentiate between classes.
Latency of inference was determined to check the applicability of the models to real-time applications. The target set at the development stage was below 50 ms for each window on the CPU. In case of ST-GCN, the latency of model inference was 2.57 ms per window, whereas the latency of full pipeline of inference with normalization and probabilities generation was 6.96 ms.
The power consumption and the memory footprint of the model on the ESP32 were not measured within the course of model evaluation process. Hence, these parameters cannot be presented as experimental results. The size of the exported ONNX model is about 73.8 KB. However, it is not the memory needed to run the model.
9.3 Comparison with Baseline Models
Comparison of the ST-GCN method with one-dimensional CNN (1D-CNN), Random Forest, and XGBoost models was done using the same 15-fold LOSO evaluation strategy. In addition to that, classification accuracy, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, ROC-AUC, and inference latency were compared where relevant.
The accuracy of the ST-GCN method was 76.50%, F1-score was 0.6578, and ROC-AUC was 0.8665 with CPU inference latency per window of 2.57 ms. In the case of the 1D-CNN model, its accuracy was 72.24%, and F1-score was 0.5190, with CPU inference latency per window of 1.14 ms.
Among the baselines, Random Forest managed to attain 76.60% accuracy, an F1-score of 0.6872, and a ROC-AUC of 0.9002. On the other hand, XGBoost managed to achieve 74.59% accuracy, an F1-score of 0.6559, and a ROC-AUC of 0.8999.
Despite outperforming ST-GCN in terms of accuracy, F1-score, and ROC-AUC, Random Forest could not be selected due to the low inference time latency of ST-GCN. This shows the balancing act that is always involved in choosing the most appropriate model for the proposed wearable stress monitoring system.
10.Result
11.Conclusion
This work offers a multimodal machine learning framework for acute stress recognition that makes use of ECG, PPG, and skin temperature readings. Based on the WESAD database, the proposed model has been designed to classify between stress and non-stress scenarios through physiological signal analysis and deep learning. Altogether, 22,192 signal windows have been used for training and testing, based on recording of 15 subjects through the LOSO cross-validation methodology.
A number of seven machine learning algorithms have been analyzed, including four deep learning models and three classical machine learning models. Among the deep learning models, the Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN) achieved 76.50% average accuracy, F1 score of 0.6578, and ROC-AUC of 0.8665. While Random Forest demonstrated slightly better results in terms of accuracy and F1 score, ST-GCN showed a good tradeoff between accuracy and latency, which was measured to be 2.57 ms per window in CPU.
The exported and validated ONNX version of the ST-GCN model showed that the maximum absolute error in the outputs between ONNX and PyTorch versions was (4.77 x 10^-6). The inference pipeline has been implemented with ONNX Runtime and shows an end-to-end latency of 6.96ms per window. This provides the software basis for real-time classification of stress levels.
In the suggested deployment approach, ESP32 is used along with physiological sensors to collect the physiological signals like ECG, PPG, and temperature, and the model execution and communication is done by the external inference gateway. Nevertheless, hardware implementation and sensor validation along with the dashboard testing still remains future work. The future work is related to integration of all hardware and software pipeline, evaluation with live physiological data, power consumption and memory estimation during deployment, etc.
